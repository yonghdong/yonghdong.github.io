Trust Region Q-Adjoint Matching: Stable Off-Policy RL for Flow Policies

A new stable off-policy fine-tuning algorithm for pretrained flow-based policies, combining trust-region principles with stochastic optimal control.

A pretrained flow policy is a useful prior, but naive critic-guided fine-tuning (e.g., QAM, QAM-E) suffers from destructive drift. TRQAM internalizes a trust-region parameter \(\lambda\) inside the sampling dynamics and adapts it via dual descent.

Large pretrained flow-matching policies have rapidly become the dominant paradigm for robotics foundation models, with vision-language-action (VLA) models like $\pi_0$, $\pi_{0.5}$, and GR00T N1 as the leading examples. Pretrained on large robot-demonstration datasets, they capture rich, multi-modal behavior priors, which makes them a strong starting point for downstream tasks.

The natural next step is to fine-tune them with reinforcement learning: take that pretrained prior and push it further with interaction data on the target task.

On a real robot, that fine-tuning has to be off-policy. Data collection is slow and expensive, every rollout takes wall-clock time, and exploratory actions can be physically dangerous. We cannot afford to throw away transitions after one update, so every transition we collect lives in a replay buffer and gets reused.

Off-policy RL on a flow policy, however, is structurally harder than off-policy RL on a more standard policy (e.g., a Gaussian or deterministic actor). The reason has to do with how a flow policy actually generates its actions.

What makes off-policy RL on flow policies difficult

A flow policy \(\pi_\theta(a \mid s)\) does not predict an action in one shot. It learns a velocity field \(v_\theta(X_\tau, \tau; s)\) and samples an action by integrating from Gaussian noise:

\[dX_\tau = v_\theta(X_\tau, \tau; s)\, d\tau, \qquad X_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \qquad X_1 \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s).\]

In practice the ODE is discretized into a chain of multiple denoising steps. The reason for going through this is expressivity: a single Gaussian policy captures only one mode of action, whereas a flow policy captures rich multi-modal distributions. This is exactly why modern robot policies have moved away from Gaussian heads toward generative ones. Most manipulation tasks admit many reasonable ways to act, and we want a policy that can represent all of them.

A flow policy produces an action by iteratively applying the velocity network from Gaussian noise. Naive off-policy fine-tuning backpropagates the terminal critic-guided loss through all sampling steps, making optimization expensive and prone to instability. (Figure style follows SAC Flow.)

The expressivity, however, is what makes off-policy RL on a flow policy structurally difficult. Modern off-policy RL is essentially Q-learning style actor-critic: a critic $Q^\pi$ is trained from replayed transitions, and the policy is improved by following $\nabla_\theta Q^\pi(s, \pi_\theta(s))$, which by the chain rule splits into $\partial Q / \partial a \cdot \partial a / \partial \theta$. For a Gaussian policy, $\partial a / \partial \theta$ is one step. For a flow policy, the action $a = X_1$ is the output of integrating the velocity field through multiple denoising steps, so each $\partial a / \partial \theta$ has to be backpropagated through every step of that chain. This is structurally the same problem as backpropagation-through-time (BPTT) in RNNs: long chains amplify gradient signals into vanishing or exploding regimes.

How prior work sidesteps BPTT (and what it trades away)

To avoid backpropagating through the sampling chain, three main strategies have appeared in the flow-policy RL literature. Each is principled, and each gives something up:

  1. Backprop with regularization (FQL). Just do the BPTT, and stabilize it. FQL distills the multi-step flow policy into a one-step policy and runs Q-learning on top. This sidesteps the chain length, but ties the effective expressivity to whatever the one-step distillation can capture — much of the multi-modal action distribution is flattened in the process.
  2. Noise-space RL (DSRL). Keep the pretrained flow policy completely frozen and treat it as a fixed mapping from noise to action. Train a small actor in noise space that learns which noise sample $X_0$ to feed in. This bypasses BPTT cleanly, but the policy’s action distribution is constrained to whatever the frozen flow policy can already produce. If the pretrained prior does not cover the optimal action, no amount of noise selection will recover it.
  3. Residual RL. Freeze the flow policy, then learn an additive residual $\Delta a$ at the action level. This corrects the destination but leaves the multi-step generative dynamics untouched — the residual cannot reshape the trajectory of denoising steps that produced the action.

Each line of work buys around BPTT by either flattening the policy (FQL), freezing it (DSRL), or correcting only its output (residual). What we actually want is something that modifies the multi-step sampling dynamics themselves, without ever backpropagating through them.

That is exactly what stochastic optimal control (SOC) offers.

Q-Adjoint Matching (QAM) and its hidden weakness

A recent line of work bypasses BPTT entirely by reformulating fine-tuning as a stochastic optimal control (SOC) problem.

The intuition for SOC is this. A pretrained flow policy generates actions through a sequence of denoising steps, starting from Gaussian noise. We want to add a small control \(u\) at each denoising step that steers it toward better actions. SOC formalizes this, and gives us a principled way to solve for the optimal control.

Now let’s make this precise. A flow policy is originally a deterministic ODE, but SOC needs stochastic dynamics, so we first rewrite the ODE as an equivalent SDE that produces the same action distribution at every denoising step (the equivalence in distribution follows from the Fokker–Planck equation). The resulting SDE (call it the base SDE, since it describes the pretrained policy’s sampling dynamics before any modification) is:

\[dX_\tau^{\text{base}} = b(X_\tau^{\text{base}}, \tau)\, d\tau + \sigma(\tau)\, dB_\tau, \qquad X_1 \sim \pi_{\text{base}}(\cdot \mid s).\]

Here \(b(X_\tau, \tau)\) is the drift (the velocity field from the flow policy that pulls the trajectory toward actions), and \(\sigma(\tau)\, dB_\tau\) is Brownian noise added at every denoising step. The trajectory starts from Gaussian noise at \(\tau = 0\) and ends at an action sampled from \(\pi_{\text{base}}\) at \(\tau = 1\).

A naive off-policy RL fine-tuning would compute the policy gradient by backpropagating through every denoising step, exactly the BPTT problem we just discussed.

SOC takes a different route. It keeps the base SDE intact and adds a learned control \(u(X_\tau, \tau)\) on top of the drift:

\[dX_\tau^u = \big[b(X_\tau^u, \tau) + \sigma(\tau)\, u(X_\tau^u, \tau)\big]\, d\tau + \sigma(\tau)\, dB_\tau.\]

The added term \(\sigma(\tau)\, u(X_\tau, \tau)\) is the control that steers each denoising step away from the base dynamics. Importantly, \(u\) is updated via an adjoint ODE solved backwards in time, so no gradient ever flows through the sampling chain.

Q-learning with Adjoint Matching (QAM) is the RL instantiation of this idea: \(u\) is chosen using a critic \(Q^\pi\), so that the controlled trajectories drift toward higher critic actions. In effect, QAM injects the critic’s signal directly into every denoising step, making the multi-step generative dynamics themselves critic-aware.

So far so good. But QAM inherits a more subtle problem: critic-induced instability.

In off-policy RL, the learned critic \(Q^\pi\) is inevitably imperfect: TD bootstrapping compounds approximation errors, especially under distribution shift. We prove (Lemma 1) that for any exponentially-tilted policy of the form \(\pi_Q(a\mid s) \propto \pi_{\text{base}}(a\mid s) e^{\beta Q(a)}\), small critic errors get exponentially amplified into large policy deviations:

\[\text{TV}(\pi_Q, \pi_{\tilde Q}) \le \frac{1}{2}(e^{2\beta\varepsilon} - 1).\]

QAM’s terminal policy is exactly of this form, so it inherits this amplification directly.

This is not just a theoretical curiosity. The QAM paper itself recognized this fragility and applied gradient clipping as a partial remedy, but empirically that turns out to be insufficient. On Robomimic, the adjoint loss still diverges past \(10^{20}\) even with gradient clipping, and as the loss blows up, task success collapses from 80% to near zero.

On Robomimic-can, QAM and QAM-E start above 80% success but collapse to near zero as the adjoint loss diverges past \(10^{20}\) (right). TRQAM stays stable on both fronts.

The takeaway is this: \(\beta\) controls a fundamental tradeoff between exploiting the critic and protecting against its errors. Large \(\beta\) exploits aggressively but exponentially amplifies critic errors (Lemma 1). Small \(\beta\) stays close to the pretrained policy and is safer, but throws away useful improvement signal. No single fixed \(\beta\) can handle both regimes, so we need to adapt it during training.

Our intuition: trust-region, but for the sampling dynamics

In on-policy RL, PPO and TRPO solved a similar tension by constraining the KL between consecutive policies. The principle: take big enough steps to learn, but small enough to avoid catastrophic drift.

We borrow the same principle, but applying it to flow policies is non-trivial. Unlike a Gaussian policy whose actions are produced in a single step, a flow policy emerges from a multi-step sampling process. A trust region defined only on the terminal action distribution would leave the intermediate sampling dynamics unconstrained. The constraint is more naturally imposed at the level of the sampling dynamics themselves.

The key technical insight: \(\lambda\) in the diffusion coefficient

Following the SOC parameterization of SOCM, we scale the diffusion coefficient by \(\sqrt{\lambda}\):

\[dX_\tau^u = \big[b(X_\tau^u, \tau) + \sigma(\tau)\, u(X_\tau^u, \tau)\big]\, d\tau + \sqrt{\lambda}\, \sigma(\tau)\, dB_\tau.\]

Consider the KL divergence between the trajectories of the controlled SDE above and the pretrained base SDE. We call this the path-space KL. Our central result (Theorem 1), proved via Girsanov’s theorem, is that the \(\sqrt{\lambda}\) scaling makes the path-space KL an exact closed-form function of \(\lambda\):

\[D_{\text{KL}}(P^u \,\|\, P^{\text{base}}) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{X \sim P^u}\!\left[\frac{1}{2\lambda} \int_0^1 \|u(X_\tau, \tau)\|^2\, d\tau\right].\]

By a simple Proposition 1, the path-space KL upper-bounds the terminal KL between \(\pi_\theta\) and \(\pi_{\text{base}}\). Combined with Lemma 1 (critic-error amplification scales like \(\beta\varepsilon\)), this yields:

\[\frac{1}{\lambda} \;\propto\; \underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}(P^u \| P^{\text{base}})}_{\text{path-space KL}} \;\ge\; \underbrace{D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{base}})}_{\text{terminal KL}} \;\lesssim\; \underbrace{\beta\varepsilon}_{\text{critic-error bound}}.\]

This shows that \(\lambda\) plays the role of an inverse temperature. Large \(\lambda\) tightens the bound, corresponding to a small effective \(\beta\) (conservative, safe from critic noise). Small \(\lambda\) relaxes it, corresponding to a large \(\beta\) (aggressive exploitation). Tuning \(\lambda\) therefore tunes the trust region directly.

Adapting \(\lambda\) via dual descent

To adapt \(\lambda\), we need two pieces: an estimator for the realized path-space KL, and a rule for updating \(\lambda\).

KL estimator. Both the controlled and base SDEs share the same diffusion term, so their discretized samplers are Markov chains with Gaussian transitions of the same covariance. We exploit this to get a closed-form expression for the per-step KL between them. Summing per-step KLs along Monte Carlo trajectories gives a path-space KL estimate, which we smooth with an EMA \(\bar D_n\).

\(\lambda\) update. We interpret \(\lambda\) as the dual variable of the KL-constrained problem

\[\max_u \mathbb{E}[Q^\pi(X_1^u)] \quad \text{s.t.} \quad D_{\text{KL}}(P^u \| P^{\text{base}}) \le \varepsilon_{\text{KL}},\]

and run projected dual descent:

\[\lambda_{n+1} \leftarrow \max\!\big\{0,\; \lambda_n + \eta_\lambda (\bar D_n - \varepsilon_{\text{KL}})\big\}.\]

This is essentially a feedback controller on \(\lambda\). When the realized KL exceeds the budget \(\varepsilon_{\text{KL}}\), \(\lambda\) rises and the sampler becomes more conservative; when it falls below, \(\lambda\) relaxes and allows more aggressive improvement. That’s the entire adaptive mechanism.

Why internalizing \(\lambda\) matters

A natural question: why not just slap a KL penalty on the loss?

\[\underbrace{\min_\theta\; \mathcal{L}_{\text{Adj-Match}}(\theta) + \lambda \cdot D_n(\theta)}_{\text{external: } \lambda \text{ as a loss-level penalty}} \quad\text{vs.}\quad \underbrace{\min_\theta\; \mathcal{L}_{\text{Adj-Match}}(\theta), \;\; \text{SDE uses } \sqrt{\lambda}\,\sigma(\tau)}_{\text{internal (TRQAM): } \lambda \text{ in the sampling SDE}}.\]

The external form makes \(\lambda\) compete with critic guidance at the loss level, so under strong critic signals the realized KL drifts far beyond the budget. The internal form, by contrast, reshapes the entire controlled SDE. The key fact is that in the SOCM parameterization we adopted, the product \(\sqrt{\lambda}\,\sigma(\tau) = \sqrt{2(1-\tau)/\tau}\) is fixed by construction, so \(\sigma(\tau) \propto 1/\sqrt{\lambda}\). Increasing \(\lambda\) therefore shrinks \(\sigma(\tau)\), which directly weakens the control contribution \(\sigma(\tau)\, u(X_\tau, \tau)\) to the drift and pulls the SDE toward the base dynamics. By Theorem 1, the realized KL is then an exact function of \(\lambda\), so the dual update enforces the trust region structurally, not softly.

This distinction is reflected in training dynamics. With external KL regularization, the realized KL drifts well above the target budget, and the task success rate degrades as the KL grows. TRQAM, by contrast, tracks the target \(\varepsilon_{\text{KL}}\) tightly throughout training and maintains a stable success rate.

Internal KL (TRQAM) tracks the prescribed target \(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0.1\) tightly throughout training on both Robomimic-lift and Robomimic-can. External KL regularization lets the realized KL drift well above the target, and the success rate degrades as the KL grows.

Sensitivity analysis

We have so far argued that \(\varepsilon_{\text{KL}}\) is a trust-region budget for the path-space KL. To check that this label is more than cosmetic, we vary \(\varepsilon_{\text{KL}}\) and ask whether the policy responds in a predictable, controlled way.

Sweeping \(\varepsilon_{\text{KL}} \in \{0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4\}\) on four representative OGBench tasks gives a clear answer:

Sensitivity to \(\varepsilon_{\text{KL}}\) on four OGBench tasks. White background is offline RL, orange background is online fine-tuning.

  1. Smooth, ordered response. Success rate changes with \(\varepsilon_{\text{KL}}\) in a predictable way on every task: the ordering across budgets is largely preserved throughout training, and the curves do not jump erratically between neighboring budgets.
  2. Tight budgets are usually best. On all four tasks above, smaller \(\varepsilon_{\text{KL}}\) trains faster and reaches higher success. The notable exception is puzzle-4x4, whose larger state space prefers a looser budget (Appendix H.3 of the paper) — i.e., the optimum tracks task structure rather than being arbitrary.

Together, these patterns indicate that \(\varepsilon_{\text{KL}}\) behaves as a controlled axis rather than an arbitrary knob: its effect on training is smooth and ordered, and its optimum tracks task structure. In other words, the path-space KL bound is not just a theoretical artifact of the SOC derivation — it is an empirically meaningful axis along which trust-region fine-tuning can be reasoned about.

Takeaways

The practical message of TRQAM for off-policy fine-tuning of pretrained flow policies.

  1. Fixed-temperature critic guidance is fundamentally fragile. Under imperfect critics, exponentially-tilted objectives exponentially amplify the error (Lemma 1); gradient clipping and similar fixes only mask this.
  2. The KL constraint belongs inside the sampling SDE. Placing \(\lambda\) in the diffusion coefficient ties the realized KL to \(\lambda\) by an exact identity (Theorem 1), so the trust region is enforced structurally rather than softly.
  3. The KL budget \(\varepsilon_{\text{KL}}\) is a meaningful axis. Its effect on training is smooth and ordered, and the optimum varies with task structure rather than being arbitrary.
